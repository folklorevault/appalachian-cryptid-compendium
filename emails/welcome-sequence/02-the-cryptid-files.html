What, exactly, is a cryptid?
A briefing before you open the drawer.
 

If you spend enough time in the woods at the edge of dark, you start to understand a simple truth: the woods don't belong to us. The light thins out. Sounds carry different. Something shifts at the corner of your eye that your brain can't file away fast enough.

You don't run. You probably don't even move. But something in you gets very, very still.

That feeling has a name, even if the thing that caused it doesn't. You're standing at the threshold of the cryptid.
Working definition — from the Greek krypto, “hidden”

If it walks through walls, it's a ghost. If it bleeds, leaves tracks in the mud, and needs to eat to survive, it's a cryptid.
Hidden, not supernatural. Flesh, blood, and bone that manages to stay just out of reach of a trail cam. Most of what's in the drawer falls into one of three categories:
The Undiscovered
Animals completely unknown to science. They look like something familiar but are fundamentally, chillingly wrong. They move wrong. They watch you in a way prey animals don't.
The Supposedly Extinct
Animals the record says died out long ago, which locals swear are still around. The scientific community shrugs. The people who live there keep reporting what they see.
The Out-of-Place
Known animals showing up where they absolutely should not be. Black panthers in the Appalachian foothills. Identified species in the wrong zip code.

But biology is only half the file. Out here, cryptid stories are also boundary markers. A creature watching the trail after dark is a line drawn in the dirt: this is where the porch light ends. The danger it stands for, whether that's the creek or the cliff or the copperheads, is as solid as the ground.

The full briefing covers cryptozoology, the Woodbooger behind my papaw's house, and why these old mountains are practically designed to hide things.
